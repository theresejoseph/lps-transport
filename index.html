The gap in the road network

Brisbane alone routes traffic through the AirportlinkM7, Clem7 and Legacy Way, with Cross River Rail to come. Vehicles do not stop needing a position when they go under: transit continuity, freight visibility, emergency response and any level of driving automation all depend on knowing where the vehicle is. GNSS needs a clear view of the sky, so it degrades in urban canyons and disappears entirely underground.

The established fix is to fit out the tunnel itself. Bluetooth beacons are specified at 20 m intervals — roughly 50 units per kilometre — wall-mounted 4 to 5 m above the roadway. Hardware alone runs US$900 to US$1,000 per kilometre before installation or traffic management, and because the batteries can be neither recharged nor replaced, the entire population has to be swapped out every four to six years inside a live tunnel. That is a permanent obligation on the road operator, renewed every few years, for every bore.

LPS moves the hardware onto the vehicle instead, and leaves the tunnel untouched.

What is LPS?

Mounted on the vehicle

On the road, nothing installed roadside

LPS is a small, low-resolution sensor that estimates position by matching what it sees against a compact reference map of the corridor. The map is built by driving the route once; every vehicle afterwards localises against it on-device. Nothing is installed in the tunnel, on the roadside or in the road surface, and the same unit works on any mapped corridor rather than only the ones that have been fitted out.

Lane-level positioning inside tunnels

Along-track position is only half of what an operator needs. Which lane the vehicle is in drives exit-ramp guidance, lane-specific traffic monitoring, incident response and automated driving decisions — and lateral separation is only a few metres, which makes it a demanding test for a low-resolution sensor. GNSS resolves no lane at all underground.

99.6% lane detection accuracy across 1.3 km with 6 lane changes


Urban driving — Kelvin Grove, Brisbane

A 3.83 km two-way urban route. GPS is available throughout, which makes this the reference point for interpreting the GNSS-denied tests above.

Median error 5.8 m · 93.9% of estimates within 25 m · cross-track RMSE 3.0 m · along-track RMSE 10.9 m

These are errors relative to GPS, not to surveyed ground truth. Low cross-track error means LPS held the correct side of the road; along-track error is larger, so how far along the road the vehicle is carries more uncertainty than which lane it is in.

How it deploys

  1. Attach the device — mount the sensor on the vehicle; no environment modification.
  2. Establish the local reference — drive the corridor once to build the map, or reuse an existing one.
  3. Localise on-device — the sensor matches live signatures against the reference in real time.
  4. Output position — a continuous position stream is delivered to the vehicle, fleet platform or app.

Where it fits in the stack

LPS complements GNSS and inertial sensing rather than replacing them. GNSS gives global position wherever the sky is visible. Inertial sensing bridges short outages but drifts over long ones, and a tunnel transit of several minutes is a long one. LPS carries positioning through the gap, drift-free, and hands back to GNSS at the portal — which makes it an addition to an existing positioning stack rather than a change to it.

Current status

This is a working research prototype with real-world results, not a productised sensor. Being straight about the boundary is more useful to an integrator than a clean claim:

  • Demonstrated Continuous positioning through 21.5 km of tunnel in both directions; lane-level detection at 99.6% over 1.3 km; 3.83 km of urban driving; indoor asset tracking.
  • In progress Portal-to-portal tunnel accuracy against a surveyed GNSS/INS and LiDAR reference, replacing GPS-relative figures.
  • In progress Day–night and adverse-weather operation on open roads; multi-lane and direction handling across a full carriageway.
  • In progress Integration interfaces and long-term map-ageing measurements, both driven by pilot requirements.

Frequently asked questions

How does this compare with installing beacons in the tunnel?

The established alternative is to fit out the tunnel itself with Bluetooth beacons, of which Waze Beacons are the most widely deployed example. Waze specifies them at 20 m intervals — 15 m minimum, 40 m maximum — wall-mounted 4 to 5 m above the roadway with at least 25 cm clearance from any metal. That is roughly 50 units per kilometre at the optimal spacing; a published Australian deployment across Transurban tunnels used more than 930 beacons to cover 18 km.

Waze publishes the hardware cost as US$900 to US$1,000 per kilometre, bought from the manufacturer. That figure covers the units alone, not installation, traffic management or tunnel access. The beacons are also consumable rather than permanent: battery life is 4 to 6 years, and because the batteries are neither rechargeable nor replaceable, a dead unit has to be replaced in full — so the whole population falls due for replacement roughly every five years, inside a live traffic tunnel.

LPS moves that hardware onto the vehicle instead. Nothing is mounted in the tunnel, nothing underground needs power or periodic replacement, and the same sensor works in any corridor that has been mapped rather than only those that have been fitted out.

How do you know it works in a tunnel if there is no GPS to check against?

At the portals there is. GPS is trustworthy on the approach and again on the exit, so each tunnel transit is bracketed by a known position at both ends, and the tunnel videos show exactly where that check is available and where it is not. That bounds how far the estimate can have wandered, but it is not a continuous measurement, which is why a surveyed GNSS/INS and LiDAR reference campaign is the next step rather than a claim we are making today.

How often does the reference map need re-surveying?

The reference is built from one drive-through and only needs updating when the corridor changes substantially. Tunnels change slowly. Because a re-survey is a single pass with the same sensor and the map is megabytes in size, refreshing a route is a routine operation rather than a mapping campaign.

Does it work at night or under changing lighting?

Tunnels are lit artificially and remain stable around the clock, which is where LPS has been demonstrated. Large lighting shifts between mapping and localisation can degrade matching; evaluating day–night operation on open roads is ongoing work.

What are the latency and update rate?

The current prototype produces position estimates at ~10 Hz, with matching performed on-device, so there is no network latency in the loop.

What compute does it need?

Signatures are ~1.5 KB per frame and reference maps are megabytes per route, so matching runs on low-power edge hardware. No GPU and no cloud connection are required at runtime.

What does it output, and how does it integrate?

LPS outputs a position stream in the reference map's coordinate frame — geo-tagged if the survey run was — which can be consumed by a vehicle system, fleet platform or application in the same way as a GNSS feed. Integration interfaces are part of current pilot work.

What happens in visually repetitive sections?

Repetition is the hard problem in tunnels: a wall 500 m in looks like a wall 3 km in. LPS resolves this by matching sequences of signatures and filtering with vehicle motion rather than deciding from any single frame. The tunnel and lane-level results above were produced under exactly these conditions.

Beyond the road network

The same sensor and the same method work anywhere GNSS does not reach. Alongside the transport work, LPS has been demonstrated tracking mobile assets indoors — a library trolley moved across five levels and left in a storeroom, and hospital equipment that has to be found rather than searched for.
