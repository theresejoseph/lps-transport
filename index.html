What is LPS?

LPS is a small, low-resolution sensor that estimates position by matching what it sees against a compact local reference map. The sensor travels with the vehicle or asset; the environment is left untouched — no beacons, markers or roadside units. A reference map covering kilometres of corridor is megabytes in size, so mapping once and localising on-device is practical at fleet scale.

These are errors relative to GPS, not to surveyed ground truth. Agreement with GPS in urban driving provides a useful reference point for interpreting later tests where GPS is unavailable.

How it deploys

  1. Attach the device — mount the LPS sensor on the vehicle or asset; no environment modification.
  2. Establish the local reference — drive the route once to build the map, or reuse an existing one.
  3. Localise on-device — the sensor matches live signatures against the reference in real time.
  4. Output position — a continuous position stream is delivered to the vehicle, asset system or app.

The reference map for kilometres of corridor is megabytes, so the whole pipeline runs on edge hardware without cloud round-trips.

Where it fits

LPS complements GNSS and inertial sensing rather than replacing them. GNSS provides global position where the sky is visible; inertial sensors bridge short outages but drift over longer ones. LPS carries positioning through the gaps — tunnels, carparks and indoor corridors — drift-free and without any new infrastructure in the environment.

Frequently asked questions

How does this compare with installing beacons in the tunnel?

The established alternative is to fit out the tunnel itself with Bluetooth beacons, of which Waze Beacons are the most widely deployed example. Waze specifies them at 20 m intervals — 15 m minimum, 40 m maximum — wall-mounted 4 to 5 m above the roadway with at least 25 cm clearance from any metal. That is roughly 50 units per kilometre at the optimal spacing; a published Australian deployment across Transurban tunnels used more than 930 beacons to cover 18 km.

Waze publishes the hardware cost as US$900 to US$1,000 per kilometre, bought from the manufacturer. That figure covers the units alone, not installation, traffic management or tunnel access. The beacons are also consumable rather than permanent: battery life is 4 to 6 years, and because the batteries are neither rechargeable nor replaceable, a dead unit has to be replaced in full — so the whole population falls due for replacement roughly every five years, inside a live traffic tunnel.

LPS moves that hardware onto the vehicle instead. Nothing is mounted in the tunnel, nothing underground needs power or periodic replacement, and the same sensor works in any corridor that has been mapped rather than only those that have been fitted out.

How often does the reference map need re-surveying?

The reference is built from one drive-through of the route and only needs updating when the environment changes substantially. Tunnels and indoor corridors change slowly. Because a re-survey is a single pass with the same sensor and the map is megabytes in size, refreshing a route is a routine operation rather than a mapping campaign.

Does it work at night or under changing lighting?

Tunnels and indoor spaces are lit artificially and remain stable around the clock, which is where LPS has been demonstrated. Large lighting shifts between mapping and localisation can degrade matching; evaluating day-night operation on open roads is ongoing work.

What are the latency and update rate?

The current prototype produces position estimates at ~10 Hz, with matching performed on-device, so there is no network latency in the loop.

What compute does it need?

Signatures are ~1.5 KB per frame and reference maps are megabytes per route, so matching runs on low-power edge hardware. No GPU and no cloud connection are required at runtime.

What does it output, and how does it integrate?

LPS outputs a position stream in the reference map's coordinate frame — geo-tagged if the survey run was — which can be consumed by a vehicle system, fleet platform or application in the same way as a GNSS feed. Integration interfaces are part of current pilot work.

What happens in visually repetitive sections?

Repetition is the hard problem in tunnels: a wall 500 m in looks like a wall 3 km in. LPS resolves this by matching sequences of signatures and filtering with vehicle motion rather than deciding from any single frame. The tunnel and lane-level results above were produced under exactly these conditions.
